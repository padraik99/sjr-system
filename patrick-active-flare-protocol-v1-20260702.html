02 — Active Protocol

Phase 1 — Active Flare Protocol

Governing Principle

Extension bias. No flexion. McKenzie centralization. Every exercise below either opens the foramen (extension) or mobilizes the nerve root without loading it (glide). Nothing in this phase compresses, flexes, or stretches tissue around the compression site. Pacing and position-cycling throughout the day is normal nervous system behavior during a flare — not a discipline failure.

03 — Rationale

Dead Hang Sequencing

Why Dead Hangs Come First

A dead hang applies gentle axial traction to the spine — the opposite loading direction of standing or sitting. That traction transiently increases intervertebral disc height and foraminal dimension, the same mechanical target the extension work is chasing, but through decompression rather than active extension. Doing this before prone lying and press-ups means the extension work that follows is happening on a foramen that's already been given a moment of relief, rather than one still under the morning's accumulated compressive load from sleep position and the walk over.

This is why dead hangs are step one, not an afterthought — they set up everything that follows in the sequence rather than standing alone.

04 — Neural Mobility

Sciatic Nerve Glide

StepInstruction
Position Supine, hook lying (knees bent, feet flat)
Movement Slow straight leg raise to the point of tension, not pain
Hold 5 seconds
Volume 5 reps each side
Side note Left side — extra cautious. Go slower, stop earlier.
Stop Criteria

Stop immediately if calf or ankle symptoms increase during or after the set. A glide should mobilize the nerve through its available range without provoking it — any increase in distal symptoms means the range or the day isn't right for this exercise. Skip it and reassess tomorrow rather than pushing through.

06 — Aerobic / CNS Track

Canal Walk Protocol

ParameterPrescription
Total distance 2 miles daily
Warmup First mile, forward, easy
Backwards walking Pole-to-pole intervals, 3 well-spaced sets
Transition 30 seconds forward-gait normalization after each backwards interval
Response so far Slight subjective improvement in gait quality; no post-activity flare
Why Backwards Walking

Backwards walking loads the spine in a relatively flexion-neutral, low-impact pattern while forcing a novel motor sequence — the reversed gait cycle can't run on autopilot the way forward walking can. That novelty demands more conscious motor control, which appears to help interrupt the guarded, pain-protective movement pattern that builds up around a flare. It's a CNS retraining effect layered on top of the mechanical benefit, not just a different way to log steps.

Progression Gate — Hold This Line

Do not add duration, sets, or intensity until a minimum of one full week of consistent non-response (no flare, no symptom increase) is confirmed. This is a data discipline, not a pace problem — one good week doesn't confirm tolerance, seven does.

09 — Daily Decision Rule

Traffic Light Response Guide

The single rule that governs every session, every day, regardless of what's scheduled:

●
Green — Leg Pain Same or Reduced
Continue as planned. This is centralization working in your favor — the nerve root is tolerating the current load.
●
Yellow — Back Pain Increases, Leg Pain Unchanged
Acceptable and often expected during centralization — symptoms moving proximally (toward the spine, away from the leg) is the direction you want. Continue, but log it.
●
Red — Leg Pain Increases
Stop that exercise or session. Peripheralization (symptoms moving distally, away from the spine) is the one signal that overrides everything else in this protocol. Back off, note what preceded it, and don't repeat that variable tomorrow without adjustment.
